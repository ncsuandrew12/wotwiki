# Note from Brandon Sanderson
In the process of editing any novel, you realize that some scenes just don't work right. There are many reasons for that. While removing such scenes is one of the hardest parts of the book writing process, it's like pruning a tree to allow new parts to 'sprout'. In the end the book gets better.
I have to say, I'm always looking for a way to show scenes like this. These pieces not only reveal what I find interesting, but often contain gems that I would like to share. The moment with Gaul and the bridge in this story is a great example.
The next scene was cut from the Memory of Light, the last book of the Wheel of Time. I must warn you, it includes a lot of characters in the middle of the arcs, so if you don't know about the Wheel of Time, you might get a little lost. I've done my best to make the scene readable on its own, but it's simply not possible to make it completely standalone due to the nature of its origin.
Also, it is not canon for the Wheel of Time. While I really like the way things are going here, our final removal decision required some changes to the Memory of Light text that included the details of this part. There is simply no place for her in the chronology of the last book. Characters will literally be forced to be in two places at the same time. Also, several minor character arcs run differently here, which contradicts the published version of the story.
This scene should not be considered as a replacement for the others. Moreover, this is a chance for me to show you something that has never been published. Think of it as a path that history could have taken but never did.
The thing is this: the enemy uses the Paths to move troops, surprisingly attacking cities. Caemlyn - the capital of Andor, one of the most important cities in the cycle - has recently been captured by an enemy using the Ways.
Our characters decided that cutting off the enemy's ability to use the Paths was vital. They cannot continue to rearm and supply supplies to the armies behind the front lines, so they create a desperate plan. Perrin, with a detachment of elite troops and guides (people using the mystical power of the Wheel of Time), go to the Paths to destroy some of the routes that the enemy walks.
I hope you enjoy this interesting behind-the-scenes material!

# P.S
One of my goals in the last book (which became three books) of the Wheel of Time was to uncover the story of the Paths. There was not much information in Mr. Jordan's notes on how to achieve this, but I felt that the text of the earlier books implied that this line would eventually come to a head.
I found such an opportunity in the need to prevent the enemy from using the Paths. Closing the Caemlyn Waygate made logistical and tactical sense. Back in the early drafts, I planned for Perrin and his squad to go to the Paths, and I could leave some hints about how the Paths could eventually be cleared.
The scene went through quite a bit of proofreading, so I received extensive comments on the revision of the scene from Harriet, Robert Jordan's widow and editor of all the Wheel of Time books, and his assistants Maria and Alan. However, the scene never went through final editing, so we decided to cut it out of the novel. Why? Well, there are two reasons.

## Problem One: Narrative Continuity and Atmosphere
This scene with the Paths came out a bit rushed during the first drafts, and therefore didn't convey the right feel and didn't have the atmosphere of previous visits to the Paths. In fact, I didn't even quite remember how rough that piece was. I thought I could get it cleaned in a couple of days of work, but it took almost two full weeks to get it in order.
There were also many small but important continuity issues. We weren't sure if Perrin would be able to navigate the Ways without an ogir guide. I think it was Maria who suggested we use the Black Sisters' notes. After all, even if Trollocs walk the Paths, there must be fairly simple instructions somewhere.
But even with this edit, the problems with this part were still present. For example, in the original version, they destroyed the Way Gate, which was described in the first books as almost impossible. In this edition, I corrected many of these problems using extensive editorial notes, but even then it may have required several more drafts before the scene was worthy of publication in the final book. The second problem, however, turned out to be more serious.

## Problem Two: Storytelling and Regression
While editing the Memory of Light, we often wondered if we looked back too often? We wanted the book to be about the battles at Merrilor Field and at Shayol Ghul. The idea was that we wanted to look ahead, not to slow down and not be distracted by any "side stories".
This scene was constantly flagged as "regressive" - it wasn't right to turn the reader's eye to The Ways (who were unimportant for the rest of the book) and Caemlyn (who had already fallen and shouldn't have been the focus after the prologue)
In the end, Harriet decided that the scene needed to be removed. We just couldn't afford 20,000 to 25,000 words about Perrin running off on his own mission in the middle of the book. This was especially true when it became clear that it would take a lot of time and edits to fix the scene - a scene that ended up being nothing more than a departure from the main plot. The tactic was smart, but in the end there was a lot the characters could do, so this particular moment wasn't particularly important.
Harriet was right and the scene should have been removed. However, it upsets me, because I like what happens in it. I like the death of Bornhald. I love the interplay between the characters and the way they come together, as well as the echoes of the early books. My favorite scene is the scene where the Ogiers appear. In the final, published version of the Memory of Light, they entered Merillor's field with little fanfare, a necessity brought in by removal.
Of all the main characters, Robert Jordan's notes are the least descriptive of what to do with Perrin. However, one of the things he said was that Perrin was to become king. I love how this scene, better than most in the published book, shows that Perrin actually managed to do something incredible - to unite disparate ideologies and ethnic groups to form a cohesive squad. I love how it resonates with the other main characters. Perrin is most suited to rule because he is capable of both building and destroying.
I would like to find a solution and refine the Paths so that they do not distract the reader. They were a vital and interesting part of the series, but ended up being ignored.
In the end, I still believe that Harriet made the right decision by cutting the scene, but I hope this post will be able to suggest an ending for one of the pattern's threads hanging in the air.
Brandon Sanderson.
